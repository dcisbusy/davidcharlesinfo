Autumn: The Sense of Reality

Greetings, human, and thank you for reading this newsletter — even though it’s probably not what you’d really love to be doing right now.

Heck, it’s not even what I’d really love you to be doing right now.

Order of Things I’d Love Us Both To Be Doing Right Now

  1. Me doing my thing outside in nature. You doing your thing outside in nature. Even better — us doing our thing outside in nature (see exciting news below).

  2. Me writing this newsletter. You reading this newsletter.

  3. Anything else.

The regular readers among you will know that we haven’t been doing much of number two on the list recently. To be fair, that’s more my fault than yours.

In fact, I haven’t written here for two months, a horrifyingly long gap that triggers a kind of guilty yearning every time I remember.

I have two excuses reasons for missing July and August:

  1. Office hours: working my ass off managing three youth expeditions to Cairngorms, Knoydart and Kyrgyzstan.

  2. All hours: working my ass off learning how to be a dad to a baby who is somehow nearly one already.

But this downward trend in publication stretches beyond the past two months.

41 > 23 > 11

For many years, this newsletter was a weekly affair, published on Fridays, for 40 or more issues a year.

That all changed when I became Expeditions Manager at British Exploring Society in August 2024.

41 issues in 2023-2024 became 23 in the year after joining British Exploring Society and subsequently only 11 since the birth of my son a year ago. Pleasingly, all prime numbers.

For most of you, this drop off in publishing volume makes not an ounce of difference to your existence. And nor should it.

But there are a small cadre of lovely people who make a financial contribution to these pages and I feel really bad that I’m not delivering on my end of the bargain.

For that reason, I have paused paid subscriptions to this newsletter until I feel comfortable enough to justify subscriptions again with consistent and high quality writing.

(Side note: as always, if you feel short-changed and would like a refund, please just email.)

Work and babies have famously made finding time to write rather difficult since the invention of writing itself.

I imagine Sargon of Akkad wringing his hands in despair at the severe slowdown in his cuneiform output during those busy years subjugating the people of Sumer and running around after his sons Rimush and Manishtushu.

(Luckily for us, Sargon’s daughter, Enheduanna, High Priestess of the Moon God and the earliest named author EVER, left us a fine collection of poetry, including the marvellous Exaltation of Inanna from which this story steals its title.)

But there is a third aspect to all this that I — along with the rest of writerdom — have only been wrestling with since around 2024.

Namely: what does it mean to write anything at all at a time when the chatbots in our pockets can generate pretty amazing stories, bespoke to the reader, at the tap of a button?

Still Standing

There’s a particular kind of gravity to Crystal Palace Park in autumn — the dinosaurs stood there through it, prehistoric and unbothered, while every year the leaves throw the same over-the-top farewell party around them, and every year I fall for it completely.

I’ll be walking a route I’ve done fifty times, half-thinking about something else entirely, and then the light comes through the canopy at exactly that low, syrupy October angle and I have to just stop, standing there like an idiot with a coffee going cold in my hand.

It’s easy to forget this park used to hold a literal palace of glass and now holds mostly ghosts and geese, but maybe that’s the whole lesson autumn’s trying to teach on repeat: that things burn down, or fall, or get quietly dismantled and carted off in pieces — and something still comes back glowing.

The challenge of AI to human writers is threefold:

  1. Do readers still care that a story is written by a human or not? Or are meatletters becoming the new vinyl, the preserve of collectors and connoisseurs?

  2. Do I, as a writer, still care enough about putting my own words down, when I could lean on a near-perfect, near-instant imitation machine to do the heavy work for me?

  3. If the answer is yes to both, then: how can I possibly prove to you that an actual human being has written all of this, when words, images and video can be parroted so precisely?

These are new questions for both writers and readers.

Although it’s hard to separate my reduction in output from the simultaneous dramatic changes to my work and home lives, I have an inkling that the rise of chatbots has indeed dealt a blow to my writing motivation.

Can You Trust Me?

Nevertheless, I have faith that there are enough readers out there who like reading the words of other humans (I am one of them) and I definitely still get a kick out of writing my own words.

Generating newsletters using a chatbot is demoralising in its good-enough-ness, but it doesn’t do for me what writing does for me — and it never could.

So that brings me to the third challenge.

This newsletter started with my friends: people who knew me in person and who could often see the seeds of my writing planted in conversations with them. I didn’t need to prove anything: the bond between words and reality was there for them to read.

Now this newsletter reaches hundreds of people I will sadly never get to meet in person. (But 👋 hello there, I hope you’re thriving.)

If I assert that human hands wrote this newsletter, there is only one way you will believe me: trust.

Unfortunately, on the internet, trust isn’t legible.

Bikes TRUE + Nationalism FALSE

Computer screens are exceptionally good at translating identity — Look at me, I’m a white British middle-aged leftie male who wears t-shirts and rides a bike in photos! — but exceptionally bad at translating character.

Yes, I’m a white British middle-aged leftie male who wears t-shirts and rides a bike in photos. You can see that in everything I post online.

My identity is there for the taking, easily translated into binary TRUE and FALSE.

  • Male = TRUE

  • Bikes = TRUE

  • Nazi = FALSE

But I’m also by-and-large honest. I’m a bit braver than I used to be, a bit more likely to take risks socially. I’m usually pretty open to your opinion, although I can get super defensive when I feel criticised. I’m somehow both selfish and love working together.

All these character traits cut across identity. Both a leftie and a Nazi can be honest or dishonest, brave or cowardly, open or defensive. Even more confusingly for the internet, real humans of all identities often demonstrate all the character traits on different days, in different contexts.

This is nothing new. Character has always muddled with identity. And we have always tended to measure our friends by their character and strangers by their identity.

What’s flipped is that, online, almost all of our interactions are with strangers rather than with friends, and it’s not only difficult to read character over the internet, it’s impossible — even if you’ve got full fibre broadband.

As a result, character has next to no value here.

(Insert sociopolitical polemic about the kinds of people who have thrived in a world where character is completely irrelevant.)

But you can trust me, yeah?

Half An Answer: Meatspace

Half an answer to the conundrum posed by chatbots is to step away from the screens and embrace the messy realities of meatspace.

It’s hard to remember a time when doing things in person was the only way to do things.

‘There was no online when I was growing up,’ as I’m sure you’re bored of hearing from people over forty.

The transition to a world where everything can be done alone, indoors, on screens, without moving has been gradual — and now sudden. It’s left us isolated and a little worried we might be irrelevant.

But I can still prove that this newsletter is real — and that the things I am writing about in this newsletter are relevant — by bringing people together, away from our screens, to share the bright reality that we can only ever find in community, in nature.

  • A chatbot will never generate for you the calm sensation of touching wood.

  • A chatbot will never cultivate a lavender bush with you, nor call a tawny owl to fly to you at twilight.

  • For better or worse, a chatbot will never make you walk down a muddy path in the rain just to catch the scent of geosmin or bring you to your first nightjar.
