Greetings, friend!

It’s October. In my local park, the pin oak and the sweetgum — both North American imports — are finally turning the most dramatic crimson.

Our long summer, with temperatures even this week touching 20 degrees, means the autumn arrivals board shows DELAYED, DELAYED, DELAYED.

The late autumn also means we’ll likely not get the most impressive colour show this year, but you can still have fun counting the leaves as they turn red, first in ones and twos on the sunny south side of the tree, then all of a sudden in bunches, flapping in the wind.

If you’re lucky enough to have a sweetgum in your local park, pick a leaf off the ground, crush it between your fingers, and breathe the scent deeply. Not for nothing it’s called the sweetgum.

“How did you get into doing this?”

Last Saturday morning, in the sunshine and under a half moon, around twenty curious humans joined me to ‘Meet The Forest’ in Crystal Palace Park.

One of the wonderful things about running events is you never know what interesting people are going to show up.

The pity for me, as host, is that I never have enough time to hear more from everyone else —

  • Like the man who did their PhD project on using the mathematics of fractal geometry to generate the skeletal structure of trees.

  • Or the man who shared that they’d spent most of lockdown at this exact spot because it held the light for longest as the sun set between two trees.

  • Or indeed the Crystal Palace Park organiser who does a sideline in lino cutting workshops in Brockwell Park.

But perhaps the moment that will linger longest is the woman who asked how I got into doing this sort of thing — spending my Saturday morning leading tree walks.

Rather immodestly, I think my answer is important — and transferable to everyone here.

Like me, the story of how I got to doing this sort of thing isn’t big and isn’t clever. It’s really rather simple:

I was open to doing something new. I talked with people, and listened. I noticed when something clicked. I reached out to others who could help.

Most importantly, I followed through.

This edition of the newsletter is the story of how those simple steps came together to create a series of walks for people in my local community.

I hope it inspires you to do the same.

Well, not exactly the same, but something else that lights you up in the way that trees do for me.

Open To Something New

I spend my days organising expeditions for young people exploring inspiring places such as Kyrgyzstan, Arctic Norway and Staffordshire.

As far as office jobs go, it’s a pretty sweet deal. But last winter I confess I was getting itchy.

At my desk indoors all day, staring into my computer screen, dislocated from the forest and the sea, gobbled up into the belly of one of the world’s biggest, baddest capital cities.

It felt a long way from my days not only organising adventures, but leading them — whether that was cycling from Glasgow to Athens with a hundred others, hiking the poems of TS Eliot, or guiding schoolkids through their first overnight camp in the New Forest.

I had to face the facts: sometimes life isn’t about endless free-range expeditions where the only worry is when we’ll next find a bakery and the only difficult decision is do we have the pistachio swirl or the cheesy burek?1

Sometimes it’s about putting down roots: changing nappies, wiping down the high chair, wheels on the bus, wendy house peekaboo, blowing kisses.

However, something in the quiet constraints of fatherhood — constraints of time, location, energy — opened me up to opportunities I never would have noticed before.

Talking, Listening, Noticing

A chance conversation: standing around in the Westow, our first time out socialising together with other new parents.

One of the dads joked that he was bored of the odd looks he’d get from restaurant patrons, as he pushed the pram up and down the high street, passing the same establishments twenty, fifty times in the hour or longer it took to get his newborn settled and asleep.

I asked him: Crystal Palace Park is right there, why don’t you walk round the park?

His reply surprised me: even before the baby came along, he’d always been scared to walk in the park at night.

Dude — that’s my favourite time to walk in the park!

In a flash, I noticed something small, local, yet adventurous that I could offer.

And if this six-foot-plus man didn’t feel comfortable strolling around our beautiful park in the beautiful darkness, then surely there were dozens if not hundreds of other people who’d never explore green spaces at night.

How thrilling to imagine I could be the one to share it with them!

Right there and then, I decided to do a night walk in the park for a dozen friends.

Following Through

I reached out to Crystal Palace Park Trust about my idea. They were generously encouraging and, I was delighted to discover, the Trust also make it as easy as possible for people to use the park for free community events.

So I registered the event with the Trust, invited a bunch of friends (mostly other new parents), wrote myself a script and then I showed up.

I was blown away that ten others also showed up.

At the Vicar’s Oak, two hours after sunset, I introduced the walk with an invitation to not shy away from our fight or flight response to darkness but rather lean into our less human and more wild selves.

Then we walked into the park together as a group, in total silence.

The distant lights of Croydon away to the south and the stars almost as close.

And I thought: wow, this works.

So I ran another night walk in February.

It rained the whole time. Most people didn’t turn up (and I didn’t blame them), but one person who did was Ruth, Senior Events Manager at Crystal Palace Park Trust.

Despite the mizzly weather, Ruth saw enough to invite me to run a third walk on the night of the spring equinox in March. This time funded by the National Lottery.

With the backing of the Trust, the equinox night walk was a sell-out — and this time entirely people I didn’t know.

The night was clear and mild: we could see 715 million kilometres all the way to Jupiter and beyond, past Gemini and Orion, over a thousand light years away to Alnilam, the central buckle on Orion’s Belt, a star many times more massive than our sun.

But the most powerful moment of the walk was when I pulled out my new owl calling whistle and, feeling more than a little silly, made cartoonish hoo-hoo noises as loudly as I could.

Just as I was turning away in embarrassed defeat, we heard him answer — a tawny owl, faint at first, calling like the steam whistle of a distant ghost train.

We stood transfixed, starry breath caught in the still air — and there, there he is! — shadow against shadow, perched high in the oak, calling right back.

That chance meeting summed up night walks for me: rare moments of animal magic shared with a small community of strangers in the fast, vast city.

Pulling On A Thread

Fast forward to October and Crystal Palace Park Trust invited me to run another three walks, including my first two daytime walks — how strange to walk in sunlight!

I decided to frame these walks as ‘Meet The Forest’ because Crystal Palace Park easily passes the three tests to meet the UN definition of ‘forest’, barring only the fact that it is obviously not a forest but a planned city park.

Yet that park contains over 3,500 trees and our short 1km walk took in more than 400 individuals across more than 40 different species.

Calling the park a forest is a dramatic reframing that brings people’s attention to how special this designed landscape is. As one of Saturday’s walkers put it: ‘I walk past these trees every single day, but never think to stop.’

My two upcoming walks on 21 November will make seven for the year, including four funded by the Trust.

I never could have imagined that a serendipitous conversation with another new dad would lead me to this point.

I still work four days a week from my desk in south London, thousands of miles from base camp, pulling together wild and remote expeditions for young people.

But Monday comes around and I know I’ve also done what my heart tells me: get outside and connect good people with great trees.

I’m proud of myself for pulling on the loose thread that dangled before me nearly twelve months ago, and grateful for the opportunity to share what I love with people in my local community, in this modern resurrection of the ancient Great North Wood.

So that’s how I ‘got into doing this’. I hope it comes across as achievable for whatever you might have in mind.

If the walker who asked the original question could’ve hung on for another five minutes, I’d have added five pieces of unsolicited advice: four encouragements and an incitement.

Four Encouragements

  1. Talk to people. I think talking to people is really the only way to identify your thing. ‘Talk to people’ does not mean social media or WhatsApp chats. I mean talking old style: you need to see the whites of their eyes. You need to believe them when they say it’s a great idea and they’d love to come.

  2. Do not wait until you think you are ready. In the words of anthropologist Clifford Geertz, we are unfinished animals. You are as ready as you’ll ever be. Having said that…

  3. Do not over-promise. Do not overreach your knowledge or qualifications — neither in your sales pitch, nor during the event. I’ve only ever got myself into a pickle when I’ve pretended to know something I didn’t — like the time I confidently declared that there was a giant sequoia in America that covered an area twenty times the size of Crystal Palace Park. Oops. (Maybe I was thinking of a clonal colony of 47,000 quaking aspen in Utah, but even that’s only half a park’s worth.)

  4. Ask people for feedback. Take criticism with a pinch of sugar and take praise to heart. We tend to lock onto what we hear as critical feedback instead of absorbing the positivity all around us. You’re never going to please everyone, so please do this for the people who love it. That should include you.

One Incitement

Sorry to break it to you, but if you want to do your thing for other people, then you’re going to need to do some amount of public speaking and sales.

If you get anxiety either standing up in front of other people or trying to convince them to come to your event, I’m here, not to tell you to give up, but to incite you to embrace both.

Don’t think of it as public speaking; don’t think of it as sales. Think of it as connecting what’s alive in you to what’s alive in them.

Two things will make these conversations easy.

First, you need to love every part of your thing.

Remember: this is your thing. You decide what goes in and what stays out. If you’re desperate to put on a triathlon but hate running, do not include running in your triathlon. People will see through you faster than a jay snatching up an acorn. So make it swim-cycle-frisbee.

Second, you need to have faith that the people you’re standing in front of are curious.

And, good news: curiosity is a universal human trait. There’s a decent chance that what first made you curious about Japanese pottery or whatever will also spark something in them. So open your mouth and tell it.

Now all you need to do is connect your passion to their curiosity.

This does not mean you need to be King Charisma. But you must nurture the (perhaps tiny) seed inside you that cherishes sharing what you love with others.

That’s all I do on my walks: first connect to the part of me that loves, say, the sinewy smooth strength of the hornbeam, then use my passion to try to reach the curious part of everyone listening.

Your passion; their curiosity.

And, like I said at the top, the wonderful thing about putting on events is you never know what interesting people will show up.

This group of people may never again gather together. So listen carefully and stay alive to what they each bring today.

Their passion; your curiosity.
